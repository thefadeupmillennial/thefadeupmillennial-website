Who Decided Some Human Lives Matter More Than Others?

The invisible hierarchy of human worth in a society obsessed with fame, wealth, status and power.

Introduction

Imagine a famous person walking into a room. Conversations stop. People stand. Phones emerge. Everyone wants to be noticed.

A cleaner walks into the same room, and barely anyone acknowledges their presence.

Both have families. Both have dreams. Both experience pain, fear, love and loss.

Yet their presence receives dramatically different responses.

There is nothing inherently wrong with being excited to meet someone whose achievements inspire us. The uncomfortable question arises when that admiration changes how we value the people around them.

When did professional achievement become a measure of human worth? When did wealth become a qualification for respect? And when did being an ordinary human being become something less than enough?

1. The invisible hierarchy of human beings

Society has many visible hierarchies. Organisations have managers and employees. Governments have leaders and citizens. Institutions have different levels of authority and responsibility. These structures can serve legitimate purposes.

But there is another hierarchy — an unwritten one that quietly influences our everyday behaviour.

It determines whose presence excites us, whose opinions immediately command attention, whose inconvenience becomes unacceptable and whose suffering is easily overlooked.

We encounter it in workplaces, restaurants, airports, educational institutions and social gatherings.

Sometimes, simply learning someone's occupation changes the way we speak to them. Discovering their wealth alters our interest in their opinions. Knowing their connections makes us suddenly more attentive.

Social psychology offers some insight into this behaviour. Research on the stereotype content model suggests that perceived social status influences assumptions about people's competence. These assumptions can subsequently shape emotional responses and discriminatory behaviour [1].

In other words, our perception of someone's position in society can influence our judgement before we understand who they actually are. But a person's position tells us very little about their kindness, integrity or moral character.

A designation can establish authority. It cannot establish human superiority.

2. The privilege of being important

Imagine two guests arriving at an establishment. One is wealthy and influential. The other is an ordinary paying customer.

The first receives enthusiastic greetings, immediate attention and exceptional patience. The second receives the minimum courtesy necessary to complete the transaction.

The distinction may appear insignificant, but it reveals something about the relationship between status and respect.

Of course, not every difference in treatment is unjustified. A person facing security threats may require additional protection. A surgeon may need priority access to specialised facilities. Someone paying for a premium service can reasonably expect additional comforts.

Equality of dignity does not require identical services, responsibilities or circumstances.

The problem begins when privileges attached to a position are interpreted as evidence that the person occupying that position is inherently more valuable.

A senior executive may carry greater organisational responsibility than an employee. That does not make their humiliation more unacceptable than the employee's.

A public figure may require additional security. That does not make an ordinary citizen's safety less important.

An accomplished artist may deserve extraordinary recognition. That does not mean the stranger standing beside them deserves any less basic courtesy.

We must distinguish between respecting a position and elevating the human being occupying it above everyone else.

3. The people who keep society running

Before a city wakes, someone has already begun cleaning its streets.

Before food reaches a supermarket, someone has cultivated, harvested, transported and arranged it.

While families sleep, healthcare workers care for patients. Security staff protect buildings. Transport workers keep essential journeys possible.

These people rarely receive widespread public recognition. Yet our dependence on their work is extraordinary.

The International Labour Organization's 2023 report, The Value of Essential Work, found that key employees earned an average of 26% less than non-key employees, with approximately one-third classified as low-paid. Many also experienced difficult working conditions and inadequate protection [2].

The pandemic exposed a contradiction in how society recognises importance. Many occupations that previously attracted relatively little prestige became visibly indispensable.

But recognition during a crisis does not automatically translate into lasting improvements in wages, working conditions or everyday respect.

This is not an argument that entertainers, entrepreneurs or highly paid professionals do not deserve their success. Nor is it a demand that every profession receive identical compensation.

It is a reminder that market value, social contribution and human worth are three fundamentally different things.

The people society notices the least may be among those it depends upon the most.

4. When visibility becomes confused with value

We live in an environment where attention is measurable. Followers. Views. Likes. Headlines. Public appearances.

Visibility has become a form of social currency.

A person with millions of followers can attract extraordinary attention with a few words. Another person may spend decades contributing quietly to their community without ever appearing in a newspaper.

But visibility measures attention, not the intrinsic worth of the person receiving it.

Research published in Psychological Science in 2016 explored the relationship between social class and visual attention. Across several experiments, researchers found that participants from higher social classes tended to pay less visual attention to other people than participants from lower social classes [3].

These findings do not establish that wealthy people lack compassion or that people with fewer resources are automatically more considerate. They do, however, invite us to consider how our social circumstances may influence whom we notice.

Perhaps one of the most revealing measures of our values is not how enthusiastically we acknowledge someone important, but how consistently we acknowledge someone who offers us no advantage.

The person who serves your food. The employee who cleans your workplace. The stranger who holds a door open. The worker whose name you have never bothered to learn.

Their lack of public recognition does not make their existence less significant.

5. The dangerous idea that dignity must be earned

Perhaps the most troubling consequence of status-based thinking is the suggestion that people must achieve something extraordinary before they become worthy of respect.

Children are frequently encouraged to become somebody. Become successful. Become wealthy. Become recognised. Make something of yourself.

Ambition can be healthy. Personal development is valuable. Excellence deserves encouragement.

But what does it mean to become somebody? Were we nobody before achieving those things?

What about someone who never becomes wealthy? Someone whose illness prevents them from working? Someone who dedicates their life to caring for a family member without earning a salary? Someone who experiences repeated failure despite sincere effort?

Are these lives less valuable?

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted in 1948, begins with a remarkably clear principle [4]:

All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights.

Notice what is absent from that statement. There is no requirement to become successful, productive, popular, wealthy or influential.

Dignity is not presented as a reward for achievement. It is recognised as something inherent in being human.

Even our appreciation of essential workers should not become another form of conditional dignity. A person does not need to be useful to society to deserve humane treatment.

You do not have to become extraordinary to justify your existence.

6. When a life becomes a headline

Consider how differently people may respond to the deaths of individuals they recognise and those they do not.

The loss of a famous person can generate widespread public mourning. Tributes appear across social media. Conversations continue for days.

These emotions can be genuine. People may associate an artist, athlete or public figure with important moments in their own lives.

There is nothing wrong with grieving someone whose work has touched you. Nor can we reasonably expect every individual to experience identical grief for every stranger who dies.

Emotional closeness and equal human worth are not the same thing.

However, we should be careful that our emotional attachment to celebrated individuals does not turn into indifference towards unfamiliar lives.

Behind every ordinary person lost in an accident, disaster or preventable tragedy is a human story.

Someone who was expected home. Someone whose absence changes another person's life forever. Someone whose plans will remain unfinished. Someone who may have been the most important person in another human being's world.

Public recognition changes the scale of public mourning. It does not change the fundamental value of the life that was lost.

The number of people who know your name is not a measurement of the importance of your existence.

7. The hierarchy we maintain ourselves

It is easy to criticise governments, institutions, wealthy individuals and celebrity culture. It is considerably harder to examine our own behaviour.

Have we ever changed our tone after discovering someone's occupation? Have we offered greater patience to someone influential than to someone serving us?

Have we dismissed a person's opinion because of their educational background while accepting another person's opinion because of their success?

Have we judged potential friendships according to someone's social connections? Have we embarrassed an employee in a way we would never dare embarrass their manager?

These are uncomfortable questions because they expose a hierarchy that ordinary people can help maintain.

We may condemn arrogance in those above us while reproducing the same behaviour towards those we believe are beneath us. And in doing so, we contribute to the very culture we claim to dislike.

Human equality becomes meaningful when it influences our behaviour in situations where kindness offers no social reward.

We should not require an audience before demonstrating respect. Nor should someone need authority over us before receiving our courtesy. The challenge is consistency.

8. Equality does not mean denying excellence

The rejection of human hierarchy is sometimes mistaken for hostility towards exceptional achievement. That is not our position.

An extraordinary musician deserves recognition. A scientist whose research saves lives deserves appreciation. An athlete who dedicates years to mastering a sport deserves admiration. A responsible leader deserves respect for their service.

Different abilities, contributions and responsibilities are realities of human society. But recognising excellence does not require diminishing everyone else.

There is an important distinction between earned admiration and inherent dignity.

Admiration may increase or decrease depending on a person's achievements and actions. Trust may be earned, damaged or withdrawn. Authority may be granted or removed. Basic human dignity should remain.

Even holding someone accountable for harmful conduct does not require denying their humanity.

A society can celebrate its most talented individuals while ensuring that people without exceptional achievements are never treated as inferior human beings.

There is no contradiction between excellence and equality. The contradiction arises when celebrating excellence requires the construction of human superiority.

9. Human dignity without hierarchy

Not everyone has equal talent, equal wealth or equal influence. But everyone has equal human worth.

At THE FADE-UP MILLENNIAL, human dignity without hierarchy is not simply a slogan.

It is a principle that challenges the way we interpret success, authority, influence and our own identity.

It asks us to admire without diminishing ourselves. To recognise achievement without manufacturing superiority. To respect leaders without treating citizens as lesser beings. To appreciate the extraordinary without overlooking the ordinary.

And to recognise that our own worth is not dependent upon the approval of someone more powerful, wealthy or visible.

We can begin practising this principle through remarkably simple choices: acknowledging people regardless of their occupation, listening without judging social status, refusing to tolerate humiliation and applying the same standards of basic courtesy to everyone.

We do not need to eliminate every social hierarchy to reject a hierarchy of human worth. We need to stop confusing the two.

Final thought

One day, the applause will end. Positions will change. Wealth may disappear. Public attention will move elsewhere. Titles will belong to someone new.

What remains when those things are taken away? A human being. The same thing that was there before the recognition began.

Perhaps the greatest sign of a mature society is not how magnificently it celebrates its most successful individuals, but how humanely it treats those who possess neither fame nor power.

Because nobody should have to become somebody important before being treated like somebody who matters.

Your achievements may distinguish you. They do not make you more human than anyone else.
