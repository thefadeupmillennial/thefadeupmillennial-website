The Business of Human Worship

How admiration becomes a commodity, loyalty becomes a marketing tool, and ordinary people's emotions become extraordinary profits.

Introduction

Imagine a young man in India earning ₹20,000 a month.

He thinks carefully before buying something for himself. He compares prices, negotiates with shopkeepers and occasionally postpones purchases because of family responsibilities.

Yet when his favourite film star releases a new movie, he purchases several tickets, contributes towards celebrations and proudly shares the film's box-office collections.

When his favourite cricketer endorses a product, he becomes interested in buying it.

When an influencer launches an exclusive merchandise collection, he worries about missing out.

These are hypothetical examples, but they represent a question worth examining.

Why does someone who carefully evaluates ordinary expenses sometimes abandon that same financial judgement when an admired personality becomes involved?

Behind the excitement, celebrations and emotional attachment exists a commercial ecosystem that understands the economic value of human devotion.

For the admirer, it may be an emotion.

For a business, it may be a revenue opportunity.

1. When admiration becomes an industry

India shares an extraordinary relationship with cinema and cricket.

Across different languages, regions and generations, films and sporting achievements bring people together. They create memories, friendships and a sense of belonging.

There is nothing wrong with any of that.

But beyond the entertainment exists an industry that understands how to convert public affection into commercial value.

A film star's popularity can help sell tickets, clothing, smartphones and consumer goods. A cricketer's reputation can become a valuable advertising asset. An influencer's relationship with followers can attract lucrative sponsorship opportunities.

Businesses are not necessarily purchasing these personalities' expertise in the products being advertised.

They are often purchasing access to the trust and attention of their audiences.

A considerable part of a public figure's commercial value can therefore originate from ordinary people who may never receive a direct financial return from that value.

Your admiration has an economic value, even when you are unaware of it.

2. The price of emotional loyalty

Consider the culture surrounding major film releases in parts of India.

Enormous cut-outs. Garlands. Milk poured over images. Firecrackers. Early-morning screenings. Elaborate celebrations organised by enthusiastic admirers.

For many participants, these activities represent friendship, tradition and collective enjoyment.

But they can also involve considerable expenditure.

Imagine someone spending ₹500 every month on activities connected to their favourite personalities.

What does monthly spending add up to?

₹500 per month

One year: ₹6,000
Five years: ₹30,000
Ten years: ₹60,000

Illustrative spending, not typical Indian fan expenditure. Assumes constant spending without inflation or investment returns.

For someone with a comfortable income, this may be perfectly affordable recreation. For someone struggling financially, it could represent a meaningful amount of money.

The problem is not spending money on entertainment.

The problem arises when spending becomes an obligation to demonstrate loyalty.

When buying one ticket no longer feels sufficient.

When refusing to contribute towards a celebration makes someone appear less devoted.

When financial sacrifice becomes evidence of being a genuine fan.

Entertainment should provide happiness, not impose a financial test of belonging.

Nobody should feel pressured to spend beyond their means to prove their affection for another human being.

3. Selling the person instead of the product

Would you purchase a particular product if an unfamiliar person recommended it?

Would you examine its quality, compare alternatives and question its price?

Now imagine your favourite actor or cricketer recommending exactly the same product.

Does your judgement change?

Celebrity advertising frequently attempts to transfer positive feelings about a personality onto a product.

An actor's cinematic achievements have little inherent connection to their knowledge of financial products.

A cricketer's sporting excellence does not automatically establish expertise in nutrition, skincare or technology.

Yet emotional familiarity can make commercial recommendations feel more trustworthy.

A 2024 consumer survey by EY reported striking findings among its Indian respondents.

Influencer marketing and Indian consumers

Follow influencers, bloggers or vloggers — 81%

Find influencer-recommended products extremely trustworthy — 60%

Have purchased based solely on an influencer recommendation or promotion — 84%

Source: EY Future Consumer Index, August 2024. Survey findings, not necessarily representative of every Indian consumer.

These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that Indians lack independent judgement.

They demonstrate why businesses recognise personal influence as a powerful commercial resource.

A famous face may introduce you to a product.

It should never replace your evaluation of that product.

Someone else's popularity is not a guarantee of your purchase's quality.

4. The illusion of a personal recommendation

Social media has changed the language of advertising.

Instead of a conventional television advertisement, commercial messages can now appear inside conversations about everyday life.

A morning routine. A family vlog. A fitness journey. A personal story. A seemingly spontaneous recommendation.

The advertisement may feel less like a corporate sales pitch and more like advice from someone familiar.

Psychologists describe one-sided emotional relationships with media personalities as parasocial relationships.

These relationships are not inherently unhealthy. They can provide enjoyment, inspiration and comfort.

However, their commercial implications deserve attention.

A 2024 academic review examining influencer marketing across 53 studies identified credibility and perceived trustworthiness as important factors influencing audience attitudes.

When familiarity becomes commercially valuable, there is an incentive to maintain it.

The personality may address millions of followers as friends or family. The affection may be sincere, but a commercial relationship can exist alongside that affection.

Recognising this distinction does not require cynicism.

It requires awareness.

When someone recommends a product online, it is reasonable to ask whether they are recommending it because they genuinely believe in its quality, because they are being compensated, or both.

5. Your attention has a price

You may not spend a single rupee following your favourite personality online.

But that does not necessarily mean your participation has no commercial value.

Every view can contribute to audience reach.

Every comment can contribute to engagement.

Every share can extend visibility.

Every additional minute spent consuming content creates more opportunities for commercial exposure.

These measurements can influence sponsorship arrangements, advertising opportunities and the perceived marketability of public personalities.

In April 2024, EY projected that India's influencer marketing sector could reach ₹3,375 crore by 2026, growing at a compound annual rate of 18%. This was a forecast, rather than confirmation of the industry's actual 2026 revenue.

Consider what makes such an industry possible.

Not merely famous personalities.

Not merely advertisers.

But millions of ordinary individuals willing to offer their attention.

This is not an argument against content creators earning a living. Creating valuable content requires effort, skill and investment.

It is an argument for recognising that our attention is valuable too.

We should be selective about where we invest it.

6. When belonging becomes something you can purchase

An ordinary shirt may become considerably more desirable when it carries the image or signature of a famous personality.

A standard event ticket may feel insufficient when an exclusive experience promises greater proximity to someone admired.

A limited-edition collection may create urgency, even when the consumer had no previous need for the item.

The product is no longer simply a product.

It can become a symbol of identity, belonging or emotional connection.

Marketing can strengthen this desire through scarcity, exclusivity and the fear of missing out.

Illustrative purchase pathway

Emotional attachment → Exclusive offer → Urgency and social pressure → Purchase

A conceptual model, not a measured causal sequence.

Owning merchandise can be a perfectly legitimate source of happiness.

But when someone feels incomplete, excluded or disloyal because they cannot afford a particular purchase, admiration has acquired an uncomfortable commercial dimension.

Your financial circumstances should never determine your right to enjoy an artist's work.

You can appreciate cinema without attending every screening.

You can love cricket without purchasing every endorsed product.

You can enjoy someone's content without subscribing to every paid service they offer.

Affection does not need a receipt.

7. The contradiction of the Indian consumer

Imagine someone visiting a local vegetable market.

They negotiate over ₹10.

They question a shopkeeper about an increase in prices.

They carefully compare the cost of everyday necessities.

Later that week, the same person might willingly pay a substantial premium for an entertainment experience associated with their favourite personality.

There may be perfectly reasonable explanations for these different choices. People are entitled to prioritise experiences they enjoy.

But the contrast raises a question.

Does our willingness to scrutinise a transaction decrease when emotional attachment increases?

Many Indian households understand the value of hard-earned money.

Parents make sacrifices for their children's education. Young professionals save towards independence. Workers send money home to support their families.

Every rupee represents something more than its numerical value.

It represents someone else's time, effort and energy — or our own.

Before making an emotionally driven purchase, perhaps we should ask:

How many hours did I work to earn this money?

Am I buying something that genuinely benefits me?

Or am I purchasing a temporary feeling of connection to someone whose commercial success is being marketed to me?

The answers may help us reconsider what deserves our financial commitment.

8. Who is responsible?

It would be unfair to place the entire responsibility upon consumers.

Advertisers, companies, agencies and public personalities also have ethical responsibilities.

Commercial influence should be exercised transparently, particularly when audiences include young people or financially vulnerable individuals.

India has already recognised the importance of such protections.

The Central Consumer Protection Authority introduced guidelines in 2022 addressing misleading advertisements and endorsements, including expectations around substantiation and due diligence.

The Advertising Standards Council of India also requires influencers to disclose relevant commercial connections, including compensation, gifts and other benefits.

Such measures recognise an important reality: personal recommendations can conceal commercial interests unless those interests are made clear.

But regulations alone cannot replace independent thinking.

Even a properly disclosed advertisement can persuade someone to purchase something unnecessary.

Businesses have a responsibility to market responsibly.

Consumers have the freedom to decide that an advertised product is not worth their money.

Both principles can coexist.

9. Becoming a conscious consumer

Rejecting commercial exploitation does not mean boycotting cinema, abandoning cricket or unfollowing everyone on social media.

It means maintaining ownership over our decisions.

Before making a purchase influenced by an admired personality, consider five simple questions.

  • Would I buy this if my favourite personality were not associated with it?
  • Can I comfortably afford it without compromising my responsibilities?
  • Have I independently examined its quality and alternatives?
  • Am I responding to a genuine need or emotional pressure?
  • Would refusing to buy it make me feel like a less legitimate fan?

If the final question makes us uncomfortable, perhaps the relationship between admiration and consumption deserves further examination.

We should never confuse supporting someone's work with surrendering our own financial interests.

The freedom to say no is an essential part of being an independent consumer.

Final thought

Somewhere in India, a parent is working overtime to support their family.

A student is saving money for education.

A young employee is waiting for their next salary.

A small business owner is trying to keep their business alive.

Each of them understands that money is earned through effort, sacrifice and time.

That effort deserves respect.

The entertainment industry deserves to prosper. Talented people deserve to earn from their abilities. Content creators deserve fair compensation for their work.

But ordinary people also deserve to enjoy the fruits of their own labour without being made to feel emotionally obligated to finance someone else's success.

Buy a ticket because you want to watch a film.

Purchase a product because it offers you value.

Attend an event because the experience brings you happiness.

Not because someone else's commercial success has become your personal responsibility.

Remember that the most important financial future you should be building is your own.

When admiration becomes a business, independent thinking becomes your greatest consumer protection.
