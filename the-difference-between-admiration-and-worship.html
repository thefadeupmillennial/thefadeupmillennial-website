The Difference Between Admiration and Worship

Admiration recognises talent. Worship asks you to stop asking questions.

The freedom to appreciate

Imagine leaving a cinema with two friends. All three of you admire the leading performer, but your reactions to the film differ. One thinks the performance was extraordinary. Another enjoyed the music but found the story disappointing. You liked the film overall, although you felt that several scenes could have been better. Nothing about this conversation requires anyone to stop being a fan.

Yet in some spaces, admitting that a favourite performer has made an unremarkable film can feel surprisingly uncomfortable. A straightforward opinion becomes a declaration of allegiance. The question changes from whether the film worked to whether the person expressing an opinion is sufficiently loyal.

This essay is about recognising that change in our everyday decisions. Here, worship refers figuratively to placing a public figure beyond ordinary scrutiny, not to religious practice or a clinical diagnosis. Enthusiasm, collecting memorabilia and attending premieres are not, by themselves, evidence of a problem. The important distinction is whether appreciation leaves us free to exercise judgement.

1. Admire a quality, not an entire personality

A talented performer can deliver a remarkable scene. A musician can create a memorable composition. An athlete can demonstrate exceptional discipline. Each achievement deserves to be evaluated on its own merits, without requiring us to reach a conclusion about every aspect of the person's character.

Try describing precisely what you admire. Instead of saying that an artist is perfect, identify the skill that impressed you: timing, preparation, emotional range or the ability to communicate an unfamiliar experience. Such specificity makes appreciation more meaningful. It also allows us to recognise similar qualities in people who receive far less public attention.

Research by Sara Algoe and Jonathan Haidt (2009) suggests that admiration can motivate people towards self-improvement. The findings do not establish that every experience of celebrity fandom has this effect. They do, however, offer a useful way to think about the positive potential of appreciating excellence. Admiration can become something we carry into our own lives rather than something we merely direct towards another person.

2. The disappointment test

Suppose an artist whose work you have enjoyed for years releases a project that does not meet your expectations. You notice that the performances feel uneven and the story fails to hold your attention. Would you feel comfortable saying so to another fan?

Healthy appreciation does not require a favourable review of every new release. Previous achievements remain achievements even when a subsequent project disappoints. A poor performance does not erase a career, just as an excellent career does not automatically make every performance excellent.

There is also no obligation to dislike a work simply because reviewers dislike it. Independent judgement means that your genuine enjoyment is valid, even when it differs from the majority. The point is not to become more negative. It is to become more accurate about your own experience.

One practical exercise is to write a private review before reading reactions online. Record what worked, what did not and why. Later, compare your opinion with others. This can reveal whether you have discovered a persuasive new interpretation or simply felt pressure to adopt the dominant view.

Admiration does not require a favourable verdict every time.

3. The evidence test

Imagine encountering two conflicting reports about a public figure. One offers an attractive explanation that fits your positive impression. The other raises questions that you would rather not confront. Neither report has yet been independently established.

A reasonable response is to reserve judgement. We need not choose between immediate condemnation and unconditional belief. We can distinguish what has been confirmed, what remains disputed and what we simply do not know.

In her 1990 review of motivated reasoning, psychologist Ziva Kunda described how the conclusions people prefer can influence the reasoning they use to reach them. This is a general feature of human judgement, not a defect unique to fans. It reminds us that feeling convinced is not the same as having adequate evidence.

Before sharing a claim, consider whether you have examined its source and whether you would accept the same evidence if you had no prior opinion of the person involved. It is equally important not to spread allegations just because we dislike somebody. Fairness requires caution in both directions. Sometimes the most responsible conclusion is that there is not enough information to reach one.

4. The recommendation test

A friend asks whether they should watch a new film. You enjoyed it, but you know that your friend dislikes its genre. What would be the more useful recommendation: insisting that everyone must see it, or explaining what you liked while acknowledging that their tastes may differ?

Admiration can enrich conversations through thoughtful recommendations. It can introduce people to new cinema, music and creative traditions. But a recommendation becomes less helpful when its purpose shifts from serving the listener to proving the greatness of a favourite artist.

A useful practice is to describe a work rather than issue a verdict about the people who enjoy or dislike it. Mention the pacing, atmosphere, subject matter or performances. Give someone enough information to make their own choice. If they decide not to watch, nothing has been taken away from your enjoyment.

This principle applies to families and friendships as much as to online discussions. Entertainment is capable of bringing people closer together precisely because people can bring different experiences and interpretations to the same work.

5. The endorsement test

Public figures often speak beyond the field in which they became successful. They may promote a product, discuss nutrition, recommend an investment or comment on a social issue. Sometimes their advice is well informed; sometimes it is not. Fame alone cannot tell us which is which.

Imagine a respected performer promoting an expensive course. Before purchasing it, consider the instructor's qualifications, the curriculum, the refund policy and whether you actually need the course. If the advertisement featured an unfamiliar person, would the offer still seem worthwhile?

This is not an argument that famous people should never express opinions outside their profession. It is a reminder that expertise has boundaries. An exceptional actor may also be an informed entrepreneur, but one achievement cannot serve as evidence for the other. The same standards should apply to advice from an unknown creator.

A practical habit is to separate the person delivering a message from the quality of the message itself. For consequential decisions, consult independent sources with relevant expertise. Appreciation need not become a substitute for due diligence.

6. The freedom to change your mind

Our tastes evolve. A film we adored as teenagers may feel different when we revisit it years later. A performance we once dismissed may become more meaningful after we have experienced something new. Changing our interpretation does not make our earlier enjoyment dishonest.

Research by Mark Leary and colleagues (2017) examined intellectual humility, or recognising the possibility that one's beliefs may be mistaken. Their studies linked this quality with openness and sensitivity to the strength of arguments. They were not studies of celebrity fandom specifically, but the concept offers a valuable principle for evaluating our preferences and claims.

We can practise intellectual humility by saying that an opinion is provisional. We might discover more information, encounter a compelling interpretation or simply develop different tastes. None of this requires permission from a fan community.

There is a difference between changing your mind because you have good reasons and changing it to avoid social disapproval. Ask yourself which process is taking place. Equally, do not force yourself to abandon a view merely to appear independent. Thinking for yourself includes the freedom to agree with others when their judgement genuinely makes sense to you.

Changing your mind is not an act of betrayal.

7. Choosing inspiration over imitation

Admiration often begins with a desire to learn. Someone watches a dancer and decides to take lessons. Someone enjoys a film and starts exploring screenwriting. Someone discovers an interview about creative discipline and develops a more consistent practice routine. These are ways of translating appreciation into personal experience.

Imitation can be useful when we are learning a skill, but it need not extend to a person's entire lifestyle. An expensive wardrobe, a particular diet or a public image may reflect circumstances that are very different from our own. What appears effortless on a screen may depend on resources, professional assistance and carefully managed presentation.

Instead of asking how to become more like a famous person in every respect, ask which habits genuinely serve your goals. Make those habits realistic for your circumstances. A student who admires storytelling might start with a short script written at home; they do not need access to a film studio to take a meaningful first step.

The value of inspiration is not measured by resemblance to its source. It is measured by whether it helps you develop something worthwhile on your own terms.

8. A personal framework for everyday fandom

There is no universal numerical boundary between strong enthusiasm and unhealthy devotion. A person may spend considerable time participating in a creative community while retaining independence, perspective and enjoyment. Another person may rarely discuss a celebrity but still treat that person's opinions as unquestionable. Context matters.

Try considering five questions when a new release, interview or endorsement attracts your attention. Can I describe what I admire specifically? Can I acknowledge disappointment without feeling that I have betrayed anyone? Can I wait for reliable evidence before reaching a conclusion? Can I let another person enjoy different things? Can I change my opinion without seeking approval?

These questions are editorial prompts for reflection, not a validated psychological questionnaire. There is no score to calculate and no label to attach to another person. They are most useful when applied privately and with patience. Everyone has preferences and blind spots, including people who consider themselves independent thinkers.

If one answer makes you uncomfortable, experiment with a small change: write your own review, decline an unnecessary purchase or listen to a contrasting interpretation without immediately preparing a rebuttal. See whether that change makes your enjoyment more relaxed and your decisions more deliberate.

Our final thought

A culture of thoughtful admiration would not be a culture without excitement. There would still be opening-night celebrations, favourite songs, enthusiastic recommendations and passionate discussions of cinema. What would change is the assumption that enjoying these things requires us to adopt somebody else's judgement wholesale.

We can recognise an extraordinary achievement without imagining an extraordinary entitlement to obedience. We can appreciate an artist's contribution while acknowledging the limits of what we know about their life. And we can allow ourselves to encounter new evidence, develop new tastes and discover excellence in unexpected places.

Admiration is not diminished by the presence of a question. It becomes more meaningful when the appreciation we express is freely chosen and grounded in what we actually value.

Enjoy the art. Learn from the craft. Keep the freedom to decide for yourself.
